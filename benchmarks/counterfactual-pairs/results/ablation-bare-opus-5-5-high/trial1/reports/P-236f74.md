# Review: Nightly Compaction Plan

The core problem is that the plan sets a fixed deadline but has no evidence the job can meet it. Cancellation is handled, but the plan doesn't say what cancellation leaves behind or what happens when it recurs.

## The feasibility gap

To finish 40 GB in 30 minutes, the step must sustain about **22 MB/s** (40,000 MB ÷ 1,800 s ≈ 22.2 MB/s) for the entire window, on one thread. That may be easy or impossible depending on the host, and the plan says throughput hasn't been measured. Things that could push it either way:

- Compaction is often CPU-bound (decompression, merge, re-encoding) or seek-bound. A single thread may not reach 22 MB/s.
- Nightly windows often overlap with backups, other batch jobs, or disk contention.
- "Roughly 40 GB" will likely grow over time, so the required rate rises every night.

There's also a timing ambiguity: does the 30 minutes include startup, reading indexes, the final write, and fsync? If so, the effective processing window is shorter.

## Cancellation semantics are unspecified

"Cancelled and reported" covers detection but not consequences. The plan should state:

1. **What state is left on disk.** Does compaction write to a temp location and swap atomically at the end? If not, a cancel mid-write could leave corrupt or half-compacted data.
2. **Whether progress is kept.** If every cancel discards all work, a job that needs 35 minutes will fail every night, and the input will keep growing.
3. **What happens on repeated cancellation.** One report per night is easy to ignore. There should be an escalation, for example an alert after 2 consecutive cancels.

## Progress logging could do more

Once-a-minute logging is good. To be useful for both operations and tuning, each line should include:

- bytes processed and bytes remaining
- current rate in MB/s
- projected completion time

With a projection, the step can **fail fast**. If it's clear by minute 5 that it won't finish, it can stop early and report, instead of using the whole window and then failing anyway.

## Recommended changes

1. **Measure throughput** on the target host (or an identical one) with representative data before enabling the job. Confirm there is a comfortable margin, roughly 1.5–2× the 22 MB/s floor, to absorb growth and contention.
2. **Make cancellation safe.** Use write-to-temp plus atomic swap, or make the job resumable or incremental so partial progress counts.
3. **Alert on consecutive cancellations**, not just single ones.
4. **Add rate and ETA to the progress logs**, and optionally stop early when the projection exceeds the window.
5. **Plan for growth.** Track input size over time, and decide in advance whether to add parallelism or widen the window if needed.
6. **Clarify the window boundaries**: what is included in the 30 minutes.

## Summary

The structure is reasonable: a bounded window, explicit cancellation, and progress visibility. But the plan depends on an unmeasured throughput, and it doesn't say whether a cancel is safe or whether repeated cancels will be noticed. Measuring throughput and defining cancellation behavior should happen before this goes live.
