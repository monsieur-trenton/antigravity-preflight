# Review: Nightly Compaction Plan

## The arithmetic checks out

- 40 GB ÷ 55 MB/s ≈ 727 s, about **12.1 minutes** (or about 12.4 minutes in binary units).
- Against a 30-minute window, that is about **2.5× headroom**. That margin is comfortable for a nightly batch job.
- With logging once a minute, you get about 12 progress lines in a normal run and at most 30 before cancellation. That is reasonable volume and enough resolution to see a stall.
- A single thread fits the measured throughput, and the plan doesn't depend on parallelism it doesn't have.

## Gaps to close before shipping

1. **Is cancellation safe?** The plan says a timed-out step is cancelled and reported, but not what state the data is left in. You need to confirm one of these:
   - Compaction writes to a new output and atomically swaps it in, so a cancel leaves the original intact.
   - Or partial output is cleaned up or resumable.

   If neither holds, a timeout could corrupt data or leave orphaned files. This is the main issue.

2. **What happens after a cancel?** Is the next night's run expected to catch up? If input keeps growing and compaction keeps getting cancelled, the backlog compounds. Define whether a cancelled night retries, alerts a person, or just waits for the next window.

3. **The throughput figure comes from one measurement.** 55 MB/s was measured once, on a production-class host. Nightly runs may compete with backups, other batch jobs, or noisy neighbours. Record the throughput each night, which the progress logs make easy, and alert when it drops sharply. Don't wait for an actual timeout.

4. **Input growth.** 40 GB is today's size. At 55 MB/s the window holds about 99 GB. Note the growth rate so you know roughly when headroom drops below about 1.5×. That gives you time to revisit the window or add threads.

5. **Reporting destination (minor).** "The cancellation is reported" should name the destination: a page, a ticket, or just a log line. A silent log entry is easy to miss night after night.

## Summary

The timing and design are sound. The plan needs explicit cancel-safety semantics and a defined response to repeated cancellations before it can be trusted unattended.
