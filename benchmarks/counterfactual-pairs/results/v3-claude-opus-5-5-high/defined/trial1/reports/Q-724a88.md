# Review: Address Backfill Geocoding Plan

## The core problem: the worker limits add up to more than the contract allows

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Workers | 12 (fixed, no shared coordination) |
| Per-worker cap | 5 requests in any one-second window |
| **Maximum combined rate** | **12 × 5 = 60 requests/second** |
| Contracted limit | 50 requests/second |
| Penalty for exceeding | Key suspended for 24 hours, which also stops live checkout lookups |

The plan's safety depends entirely on per-worker limiters, and those limiters together permit 60 req/s. That is 20% over the contract. Nothing coordinates the workers, so nothing stops all 12 from running at their cap at the same moment. With 3.2M rows of steady, throughput-bound work, they will do exactly that for most of the run. A single over-limit window is enough to trigger suspension, so this is close to certain to happen, not a rare edge case.

The other details in the plan are sound, but none of them close this gap:
- **Retries share the limiter.** Good. Retries cannot push a worker above 5/s. They still count toward the 60.
- **No other users of the key.** Good. This removes outside traffic, but the backfill exceeds the limit by itself.
- **Worker count fixed.** Good. The total stays predictable, and that predictable total is 60.

## Required changes (any one fixes the violation)

1. **Lower the per-worker cap.** Use 4 req/s per worker, giving 12 × 4 = 48 total. This is the smallest change.
2. **Reduce the worker count.** 10 workers × 5 = 50 is exactly at the limit with no headroom, so I'd advise against it. 9 workers × 5 = 45 is safer.
3. **Use a shared limiter.** For example, a Redis token bucket set to about 45 req/s across all workers. This makes the global cap explicit instead of relying on arithmetic, and it stays correct if the worker count ever changes.

## Recommended hardening

- **Leave headroom below 50.** The vendor may measure windows differently from your clients (fixed vs. sliding windows, clock skew, network batching that lands requests in the same second). A suspension here is very costly, so target 40–45 req/s rather than 48–50.
- **Handle 429 and rate-limit responses.** On any rate-limit response, back off globally instead of retrying.
- **Add a kill switch and monitoring.** Track the observed aggregate request rate, and be able to halt the job quickly.
- **Check the runtime.** At 45 req/s, 3.2M rows takes about 19.8 hours. At 40 req/s it takes about 22 hours. Schedule accordingly, and consider checkpointing so the job can resume.
- **Consider a separate API key** for batch work, if the vendor allows it. A batch-job mistake would then not take down checkout.

## Summary

As written, the plan allows 60 req/s against a hard 50 req/s limit. That breaks the requirement it states and puts checkout address lookups at risk of a day-long outage. Lowering the per-worker cap to 4 or below, or adding a shared global limiter with headroom, would make the plan acceptable.
